## 3. Ukraine's presidential office head says European security without the US would be 'very dangerous'
*World & Geopolitics · Euronews*

Kyrylo Budanov, head of Ukraine's Presidential Office, told Euronews that a European security architecture without US participation would be "very dangerous for Europe." He said Russia is carrying out intensive aerial attacks that kill civilians, and that Ukraine is testing new jet-powered drone interceptors in combat, with the challenge now being scaling production. He added that several European countries have signed defence cooperation agreements with Kyiv under a "Drone Deal" framework and that financing talks continue with the IMF and the European Commission.

## 4. arXiv caps submissions at two per month per author as paper volume doubles
*AI & Tech · arXiv Blog*

arXiv announced an immediate rate limit of two submissions per calendar month and three active submissions at any one time for each submitter, across all subject areas. September 2026 brought 40,363 submissions, double the volume of two years earlier, with cs.AI growing sixfold over the period. arXiv attributes the surge to AI tools that make it easy to produce low-value papers, and says a small share of authors consume a disproportionate amount of volunteer moderator time. Rejected papers count toward the monthly limit, and limits apply to the submitter rather than co-authors.

## 5. Physicists find electrons moving collectively in 'quantum slow motion' in a layered magnet
*Science · ScienceDaily (University of Chicago, Science Advances)*

Researchers at the University of Chicago Pritzker School of Molecular Engineering report an unexpected quantum state in the layered magnetic material Fe5GeTe2, in which large numbers of electrons move together extremely slowly while staying quantum coherent. The material shows a flat electronic band, and the coherent behavior persisted up to about 100 degrees above absolute zero, which is relatively high for quantum materials. The results, published in Science Advances, deviate from theoretical predictions of the material's magnetic interactions. The authors suggest possible uses in memory devices if the effect can be made to work at room temperature.
